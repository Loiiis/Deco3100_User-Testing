Who can buy the house first in Sydney?

How social class shapes home ownership opportunities

Rising house prices affect everyone, but not everyone starts from the same place.

Introduction

Sydney is one of Australia’s most economically prosperous cities, yet significant social class inequalities remain. Access to education, income, and housing opportunities is often shaped by social class (ABS, 2021; Weber, 1978; Bourdieu, 1986).

At the same time, housing prices in Sydney have risen substantially over recent decades, making home ownership increasingly difficult for younger generations (Property Carto, 2026).

But not all young people face the same challenges.

By examining socioeconomic indicators and housing data across Sydney, this website explores:

How social class continues to influence who can buy a house first in contemporary Australia.

Part 1: Sydney's Housing Crisis

Why are young Australians finding it harder to buy a home?

Housing prices in Sydney have risen substantially over the past several decades, significantly outpacing growth in household incomes and contributing to declining housing affordability among younger generations.

Research also shows that parental financial support has become increasingly important for young Australians entering the housing market (Parkinson et al., 2019). Young Australians today are often more dependent on parental assistance to purchase a home than previous generations.

However, not everyone receives the same financial assistance from their parents.

Families from more affluent socioeconomic backgrounds are typically able to provide more financial support, while disadvantaged families usually have fewer resources.

This raises an important question: Do all young people face the same challenges in buying a home?

Part 2: Different Starting Points

Do all young people face the same challenges in buying a home?

IRSAD (Index of Relative Socio-economic Advantage and Disadvantage) is a composite index developed by the Australian Bureau of Statistics to measure the relative socioeconomic conditions of an area.

It combines indicators such as: income, education, employment, and occupation to identify areas with higher levels of advantage or disadvantage(ABS, 2021).

Quick Guide to IRSAD

Higher IRSAD scores indicate greater socioeconomic advantage, while lower scores indicate greater socioeconomic disadvantage.

Does a higher IRSAD score correspond to higher educational attainment?

💡 Each dot represents a Sydney suburb. Hover to view suburb-level data.

📌 Areas with higher IRSAD scores generally have a much higher proportion of residents holding a bachelor degree or above.

Residents in socioeconomically advantaged areas are more than twice as likely to hold a bachelor degree or higher qualification than residents in disadvantaged areas.

Higher educational attainment is associated with better employment opportunities and higher incomes, increasing the capacity to enter the housing market (ABS, 2021).

💡 Hover to compare IRSAD scores with homeownership rates across suburbs.

📌 Socioeconomically advantaged areas also tend to have higher rates of home ownership.

Educational attainment and income explain part of the inequality in homeownership.

However, research suggests that family background continues to matter even after individuals achieve similar educational qualifications and earnings (Mendolia & Siminski, 2016).

This means family background can provide a significant advantage when purchasing a home.

If abilities are equal but family backgrounds differ, how does this affect homeownership?

Part 3: Family Background Matters

When ability is equal, does family wealth matter?

The research shows that intergenerational advantage, such as gifted deposits, will be a big help for youth who need to buy a house (Lovenheim & Reynolds, 2013).

Key Takeaway

In Sydney, home ownership is shaped not only by individual effort, but also by social class, education, and intergenerational wealth.

Conclusion

In Sydney, young people from socioeconomically advantaged families are generally able to purchase homes earlier than those from disadvantaged backgrounds.

While rising house prices affect all Australians, access to education, income, and intergenerational wealth provides some groups with a significant head start.

In contemporary Sydney, home ownership is shaped not only by individual effort, but also by the social class into which a person is born.

Therefore

Understanding housing inequality requires more than just focusing on individual effort. Homeownership affordability depends not only on individual achievement but also on the unequal starting lines created by class inequality.

As housing affordability continues to decline, addressing these inequalities will remain a significant challenge for Australia in the future.
